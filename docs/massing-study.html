01

What the primary source says that the brief does not

Six points from reading the Afaan Oromo instrument directly. Four of them move area; two of them move the massing.

Art. 8.2(6) — the departmental rooms are 96 m² in total

"Kutaalee muummeewwanii 4 bal'inni waliigalaa isaanii kaaree meetira 96 (8m x 12m) ta'ee…"

The brief flags this as ambiguous and adopts 96 m² each. The word waliigalaa means combined, and the parallel provision for secondary settles it: Art. 8.3(5) gives four departmental rooms at 120 m² total and 30 m² each. Total-then-each is the Bureau's own drafting pattern, and it is content with 30 m² departmental rooms at secondary level.

Consequence: −288 m² net, roughly −400 m² GFA. Still worth confirming, but the ambiguity now runs in our favour rather than against.

Art. 15 — ask for much less, and you may not need a waiver at all

The brief seeks blanket consolidation and prices refusal at 1,809 m². Art. 15 requires only that co-located schools bulchiinsa ofii isaanii akka qabaatan — maintain their own administration. That is an administrative unit, not a duplicated building.

A swing of about 2,330 m² of GFA — most of a storey — and it narrows the project's regulatory dependency to the Art. 6 compound-area waiver alone. Art. 11(4), read carefully, sets minimum enrolments for opening a new school, not caps.

The sanitation is the unpriced problem

Arts. 8.2(13) and 8.3(17) specify boolii — pits — at one per 25 students. Pits cannot be stacked seven storeys. This is necessarily full waterborne sanitation: about 144 WCs, 25–40 m³ of effluent a day, and no room on 4,621 m² for a septic tank and soakaway.

Affects the massing now

The answer is an on-site packaged plant — a baffled reactor with a planted gravel filter — with treated effluent irrigating the Art. 7(4) agricultural terrace and flushing the WCs. That needs land or basement volume reserved at concept stage, not at services stage. It is also the strongest environmental argument the scheme has, and it is a necessity rather than a gesture.

Water storage is a structural load

Two days' storage for 3,600 students on waterborne sanitation is 70–100 m³ — up to 1,000 kN, concentrated on the roof. The brief lists "plant, water storage" without a number. Split it: bulk suction tank at the base, a 20–25 m³ service tank at roof, pumped.

The five Golee are a plan diagram, not furniture

Art. 17(1) requires every KG classroom to be divided into five learning corners — language, mathematics, science, social and cultural, art and handcraft. Thirty children plus five corners in 63 m² is 2.1 m² per child. They will not fit inside the room.

They fit as a shared commons: five classrooms opening onto one Golee commons that holds the five corners at proper size. Four such clusters make 20 classrooms — and four clusters is exactly the four KG units Art. 11(1) would have us register. The registration structure and the pedagogical structure become the same thing, built rather than waived.

The metric that should drive the massing is not land per student

The brief's headline figure — 1.28 m² of land per student — measures the problem. It does not measure the solution. What the vertical typology destroys, and must then manufacture back, is usable outdoor area.

Terracing does not add outdoor space here. It recovers the ground the building consumed, almost exactly once over. So every option below is judged first on how much contiguous, usable, shaded deck it generates — and only then on how it looks.

03

The tyranny of thirty-two by nineteen

A FIBA court plus run-off is 32 × 19 m. The 19 m clear dimension governs this project's massing more than anything else, because a classroom wing is 17–18 m deep — seven metres, a corridor, seven metres. A court will not sit on the roof of a classroom wing. It needs one of three things: the roof of a deeper single-storey volume, the top of the building, or a 19 m setback taken in one move at a cost of 600–1,200 m² of plate.

That single fact eliminates two of the four massing families outright, and it is why the brief's Level 1 sports terrace costs 900 m² of the plate that would otherwise make a courtyard possible.

So invert the brief's sport and greenery stacking

Brief 01, §8
  • Level 1 — 900 m² court and hard play
  • L2–L6 — greenery, agriculture, outdoor teaching
  • Roof — plant, 130–150 m running loop
Proposed inversion
  • Level 1 — soft play deck for kindergarten, 280 m²
  • L3–L5 — agriculture, outdoor classrooms, study terraces
  • Roof — full 32 × 19 court and 170 m running loop
  • Court sport is a timetabled, supervised, secondary-weighted activity, and the secondary school sits at L5–L6. Putting the court on the roof puts it closer to its users, not further.
  • Young children need frequent, brief, adjacent outdoor access — a 280 m² soft deck they can spill onto in four minutes, not one hard court five floors below.
  • The roof plate takes the court and the running loop at no cost to any classroom floor. The same court at Level 1 costs 900 m² that must then be rebuilt higher or wider.
  • The impact-noise problem the brief identifies simply disappears. Nothing sits beneath the roof court but plant.

The cost is the 4–6 m ball-catch enclosure and lift access for equipment. The brief already wants the enclosure designed as a taut faceted volume — the most visible thing on the skyline. On the roof it becomes the building's crown rather than a fence halfway up its side.

05

The recommended scheme — an eroding courtyard

A ring around a central void, from which single large notches are removed as the building rises, spiralling around the four faces.

  • Outline 56 × 52 m, courtyard 32 × 14 m (448 m²)
  • NE wing, 18 m deep (7 + 4 + 7) — classrooms to the road and the court
  • SW wing, 20 m deep (7 + 6 + 7) — a six-metre learning street on the hot afternoon face, doubling as thermal buffer
  • End wings, 12–14 m — three cores, stacked sanitary, specialist rooms

Why it holds together

The court sets the wing depth, and the wing depth buys the learning street. Needing 19 m clear pushes the south-west wing to 20 m, which yields a six-metre daylit circulation spine. Design Principle 5 — corridors as informal learning area — arrives as a consequence of geometry rather than as an act of generosity that the gross-up factor then has to absorb.

Few large notches, not many thin ones. A 14 × 14 m deck is an outdoor classroom for fifty children. A 1.5 m ledge is nothing at all. Each notch also self-shades the facade two floors beneath it, and the spiral gives the silhouette its identity without any geometry that costs money to build.

No point on any floor is more than 9 m from daylight, from either the outer facade or the courtyard, and every classroom is double-aspect. Every column runs uninterrupted to foundation.

The concept name comes from the regulation

Art. 2(12) defines Gaaddisa Dubbisaa — a shaded outdoor reading place under trees with good air. The courtyard should carry that name and that job. Roofed at high level with an open-sided louvred canopy, the void becomes a genuine solar chimney driving stack ventilation; the flag-ceremony space of Art. 7(2), usable through the kiremt rains; a 450 m² rainwater catchment feeding the agricultural terrace; and the one space every student passes through daily, overlooked by every corridor balcony — Principle 8, passive supervision, delivered by the plan rather than by cameras.

Seven 8 m bays make 56 m — the full wing length, fourteen classrooms per full floor, 85 classrooms across six upper levels. Columns land on partition lines and in the circulation zone, never mid-room. That matters most at Level 1, where columns sized for seven storeys arrive at 900–1,200 mm square inside kindergarten accommodation that Art. 8.1 leaves no spare area in.

06

On orientation — a respectful disagreement

The brief calls rotating the dominant axis toward east–west "the single most valuable move available to the design." At 9° North I would weight this differently — and would test it rather than assume it.

The plot's edges run at roughly 45° to the cardinals. Rotating a building to true east–west inside a 45°-skewed corner site leaves triangular residual land on all four sides, which at 1.28 m² per student is the least affordable geometry available. Meanwhile the solar penalty of the site's natural grain is smaller than temperate intuition suggests: at this latitude the noon sun sits at 65–85°, and the governing problem is the low east and west sun at 10–30°, which no plan rotation solves. Only section does.

Proposition

Hold the plan to the site grain. Solve solar in section. Classrooms face north-east and south-east — the better orientations — and the courtyard. The twenty-metre south-west wing puts its learning street, stairs and veranda on the hot afternoon face as a thermal buffer, not a habitable room. The road elevations take the dust and the noise and get the taut screened face; the quiet residential elevations get the cascading planted notches and the view to Wechecha.

An orientation study comparing the site-grain scheme against a rotated one is a Phase 1 deliverable either way. I would simply not presume the answer before drawing both.

What I need before Massing 02

The topographic survey and boundary dimensions

Plus the Ambo Road reservation. Every plate figure on this page is parametric until this lands — the 56 × 52 m rectangle is a working assumption derived from the area, not from a drawing.

A decision on the sport inversion

It is the move that unlocks the courtyard. Keep the court at Level 1 and it consumes the 900 m² of plate that makes the courtyard possible.

Is a basement viable?

Brief 01 never raises one. A single 2,400 m² basement absorbs the water tanks, the treatment plant, plant rooms, bulk stores, kitchen support and staff parking — releasing 500–800 m² of ground floor back to teaching and play. At this density it may be the cheapest area on the site. It needs the geotechnical report and the water table before it can be priced.

Permitted site coverage and setbacks

The scheme sits at 63% outline coverage. If the local plan caps it at 50%, this study restarts.
